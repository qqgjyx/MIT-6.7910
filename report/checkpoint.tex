\title{Similar Predictions, Different Weights:\\How AdamW and Muon Train a Small Language Model\\[1pt]
\normalfont Initial checkpoint, MIT 6.7910, Fall 2026}
\author{Juntang Wang and V\'ictor Conchello}
\date{November 1, 2026}

\maketitle
\vspace{-0.8em}

\paragraph{Summary.} We train a small language model with AdamW and Muon from one initialization, on the same
mini-batches. (1)~Muon trains faster, and on a slightly different path. The
final models are about as far apart as two data orders, but Muon's loss is lower. (2)~The optimizers differ in various metrics
that look at the model's internals: weight spectra, how alike consecutive updates are, curvature even at similar step
sizes, and MLP sparsity. (3)~When a run switches optimizer mid-training, its updates change at once. For its weight spectra and MLP
sparsity, the earlier the switch, the closer they end to the second optimizer's own run; the same holds for sharpness
after a switch to Muon. Even a
switch at step 200 leaves a trace.

\paragraph{Setup.} \emph{Model.} We train a 14M-parameter nanoGPT \citep{karpathy2022nanogpt} on TinyStories
\citep{eldan2023tinystories} for 10{,}000 steps of 128 sequences of 256 tokens
(\cref{app:hparams} lists the model, data, training and optimizer settings).

\emph{Optimizers.} Muon \citep{jordan2024muon} orthogonalizes the update of each weight matrix in the transformer blocks: it sets the
update's singular values to about 1. AdamW trains Muon's other parameters. Each optimizer uses the best of five
learning rates. All runs start from the same weights. Each optimizer runs on the same five \emph{data orders}.

\paragraph{Step 1: how the trajectories differ (\cref{fig:div}).} \emph{Comparing runs.} The distance between two runs is the mean
Jensen--Shannon divergence (JS, in bits) of their next-token predictions on held-out text. We compare runs at equal
step and at equal training loss; at equal loss, speed does not count as a difference. The \emph{floor} is the mean distance between two data
orders of one optimizer. A distance of one floor means ``as different as two
data orders''.

\caption{AdamW and Muon. (a) Checkpoints from step 250 on, mapped to 2-D by densMAP (shows neighbours, not

Validation loss.}
\label{fig:div}

\emph{Faster, and on a slightly different path.} Muon reaches AdamW's final validation loss, 1.344, by step 7500 (d). Since they share the
initialization and batches, if Muon just followed AdamW's path faster, they would be closer at equal loss (c) than
at equal step (b). The opposite happens: from step 250 on, their distance is
larger at equal loss than at equal step (at step 1000, 0.131 against 0.102). So the paths differ, but less than a change of
data order does: from step 500 on, at equal step, AdamW and Muon on the same data order are closer than two data
orders of one optimizer (a, b).

\emph{Endpoints.} The final AdamW and Muon models are about as far apart as two runs of one optimizer on different
data orders (b), but Muon ends at a lower validation loss: 1.309 against 1.344 (d).

\paragraph{Step 2: what differs along the way.} We measure 30 properties of each model at 34 points of training, in
five groups: weights $W$, one-step updates $\Delta W$, loss curvature (Hessian $H$), internal activations and
predictions (\cref{app:metrics} defines each metric and compares all of them in \cref{tab:app-am,tab:app-sgd}).
\Cref{tab:app-sgd} also gives the values for SGD with momentum.

We compare the optimizers by each metric's ratio AdamW/Muon at training loss 1.81, on three data orders each
(\cref{fig:rank}, \cref{tab:app-am}). The $\pm$ gives its range over data orders; if it excludes 1, the optimizers
differ more than data orders do.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics{figures/ts10m/ranking.pdf}
\caption{Ratio AdamW/Muon at training loss 1.81, for selected metrics; three data orders each; bars: range across
data orders.}
\label{fig:rank}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Weights $W$.} Muon's weight matrices have more evenly sized singular values. Their stable rank
$\|W\|_F^2/\|W\|_2^2$ is 86 against AdamW's 33 (Muon/AdamW $2.61\pm0.04$), and their effective rank 325 against 272. Their
spectral norm (2.9 against 5.4) and largest-to-median singular-value ratio $\sigma_1/\sigma_{\rm median}$ (2.7 against
8.0) are lower. Both move similarly far from the initialization (ratio $1.01\pm0.02$): they differ in the shape of
their weights, not in how far they travel.
\item \emph{One-step updates $\Delta W$.} Consecutive Muon steps are less alike (cosine 0.74 against 0.88). Their stable rank is much
higher (220 against 17; \cref{tab:app-am}), but by construction: Muon orthogonalizes every update.
\item \emph{Loss curvature (Hessian $H$).} Muon is about twice as sharp: the top Hessian eigenvalue $\lambda_{\max}$ is 14 against 6.2,
the trace $\operatorname{tr}H$ 2770 against 1400. This holds even though both take steps
of similar relative size ($\|\Delta\theta\|/\|\theta\|$ is $6.53\cdot10^{-3}$ for Muon, $6.56\cdot10^{-3}$ for AdamW), so it is not
an artifact of their different learning rates.
\item \emph{Internal activations.} AdamW's MLP is sparser: 12\% of its units are active, against 22\% for Muon.
\item \emph{Predictions.} No prediction metric separates the two: every AdamW/Muon range contains 1 (\cref{tab:app-am}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Step 3: swaps (\cref{fig:swap}).} \emph{Swaps.} A \emph{swap} switches a run of one optimizer (the
\emph{parent}, on data order 0) to the other optimizer at a \emph{fork step}, then trains it to step 10{,}000 on the
parent's batches (\cref{app:forks} gives the fork steps, the optimizer state at the swap and the measurement steps).
An optimizer's \emph{own run} is its run from the start. We swap at nine fork steps from 50 to 4000, in both
directions. We give each metric's value at step 10{,}000 as a fraction of the way from the parent's value (0) to the
own run's (1).

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/ts10m/swap_traj_adamw_muon.pdf}
\caption{Swaps on data order 0: five metrics against the step. Top: AdamW to Muon; bottom: Muon to AdamW. Black: the
parent's optimizer without a swap; grey dashed: the new optimizer's own run; colour: swapped runs, by fork step (dot:
the swap).}
\label{fig:swap}
\end{figure}

\emph{Weights $W$ and internal activations.} For the spectral norm and stable rank of $W$ (a, b, f, g) and the
fraction of active MLP units (e, j), the final value depends on the fork step: the earlier the swap, the closer it ends to the new optimizer's own run. Even a
swap at step 200 leaves a clear trace: from AdamW to Muon, the stable rank ends 0.90 of the way and the active fraction 0.67. This
points to the importance of the early steps.

\emph{One-step updates $\Delta W$.} The cosine between consecutive updates moves to the new optimizer's level right
after the swap (c, h). This is expected: it reflects the optimizer's own dynamics more than the model's weights.

\emph{Loss curvature (Hessian $H$).} $\lambda_{\max}$ differs by direction (d, i). After a switch to AdamW, it
falls to AdamW's level (1.00--1.18 of the way), often after a brief spike. After a switch to Muon, it behaves like the
stable rank and active units above: the earlier the swap, the closer it ends to Muon's own run, and late swaps keep
much of AdamW's value.

\paragraph{Next steps.} (i)~Repeat Steps 1--3 on a larger model (TinyStories-30M) and on character-level Shakespeare.
(ii)~For each property that differs in Step 2: does it follow directly from Muon's orthogonalized updates, or does it
emerge during training? (iii)~Do these
differences explain Step 1, Muon's faster training and different path? Is any of them a reason Muon does better?
