\author{Juntang Wang and V\'ictor Conchello}
\date{November 1, 2026}

\paragraph{Summary.} We train one transformer from one initialization with AdamW and with Muon
\citep{jordan2024muon}, on the same mini-batches. (1)~At equal training loss, AdamW and Muon
predict as similarly as two runs of one optimizer on different data orders; the loss barrier between their final
weights exceeds data-order barriers in only one of three settings. (2)~The singular values of
their weight and update matrices differ beyond data-order variation (Muon's more evenly sized, AdamW's dominated by a few
large ones), while their prediction statistics do not. (3)~A run switched to the other optimizer
mid-training ends, in its predictions, no further from either optimizer's own run than two data orders are; its
updates take the new optimizer's form, but its weights and activations keep part of the old one's.

\paragraph{Setup.} The main setting, TinyStories-10M, trains a 14M-parameter nanoGPT
\citep{karpathy2022nanogpt} (10.6M without embeddings and head) on TinyStories
\citep{eldan2023tinystories} for 10{,}000 steps of 128 sequences $\times$ 256 tokens with a cosine learning-rate
decay; two more settings, a 30M model on TinyStories (TinyStories-30M) and a character-level model on Shakespeare,
serve as checks (\cref{app:hparams}). Muon orthogonalizes the update of each weight matrix in the blocks
(sets its singular values to about 1); a separate AdamW trains the embeddings, output head and LayerNorm gains;
each optimizer uses the best of five learning rates tried. All runs start from the same initial weights. A
\emph{data order} is a fixed choice of mini-batch for every step; each optimizer is run on five. The distance between
two runs is the mean Jensen--Shannon divergence (JS, in bits) of their next-token predictions on 2{,}048 fixed
held-out positions. We compare two runs at the checkpoints where they reach the same \emph{training loss} (on 64
fixed training sequences), so that a faster optimizer is not counted as different, and divide their distance by a
\emph{floor}: the mean distance between two data orders of one optimizer (for two optimizers, the larger of their
floors). A distance of about one floor thus means ``as different as two data orders''. For any other metric, we compare
two optimizers by the \emph{ratio} of their values at equal training loss, with a margin over data orders: if the
first optimizer's values range over $[a_{\min},a_{\max}]$ and the second's over $[b_{\min},b_{\max}]$, the ratio lies
in $[a_{\min}/b_{\max},\,a_{\max}/b_{\min}]$, written as midpoint $\pm$ half-width; an interval that excludes 1 means
the optimizers differ by more than data orders do. The \emph{loss
barrier} between two weight vectors is the largest amount, over 11 evenly spaced points on the straight line between
them, by which the training loss exceeds the straight line joining its two end values \citep{frankle2020lmc}; with a
near-zero barrier they are \emph{linearly connected}. Losses and barriers are in nats. In a \emph{swap}, a run of one
optimizer (the \emph{parent}, data order 0) is switched to the other at a \emph{fork step} and trained to step
10{,}000 on the parent's batches; the new optimizer starts from a \emph{warm state}, its momentum statistics gathered
alongside the parent at learning rate 0. Its \emph{own run} is the run trained with it from the start. In total we
trained 141 runs using 33 GPU-hours (\cref{app:compute}).

\paragraph{Step 1: how the trajectories differ (\cref{fig:div}).} After the learning-rate warmup, AdamW and Muon
runs on the same data order stay close: each such pair are nearest neighbours in the densMAP plot (\cref{fig:div},
left), and at every step their JS is smaller than between two data orders of one optimizer (second panel). Muon learns
faster (third panel): by step 7500 it reaches AdamW's final validation loss, 1.344. Compared at equal training loss, the AdamW--Muon distance is about
equal to the floor: at training losses below 4.85 it is 0.95--1.38 times the floor (median 1.02), and 0.99 times at
the lowest loss all runs reach (1.02 on each of TinyStories-30M and Shakespeare). So, judged by their predictions,
AdamW and Muon end as similar as two runs of one optimizer on different data orders.

Their final weights, however, are further apart than two data orders' are, at least on TinyStories-10M
(\cref{fig:div}, right): the loss barrier is 3.5--4.1 between AdamW and Muon, against 1.2--1.9 between two data orders of AdamW and 1.6--1.8 between two
of Muon. On TinyStories-30M and Shakespeare, the mean AdamW--Muon barrier lies between the two optimizers' mean
data-order barriers (4.64 between 1.78 and 5.05; 2.42 between 0.14 and 4.34).

\paragraph{Step 2: what differs along the way.} We measure 30 properties of each model at 34 points, from the first
steps to the final model (\cref{app:metrics}). They form five groups: the weights, the
change made by one update step, the loss curvature, internal activations, and the
predictions. We compare optimizers by each metric's ratio at training loss 1.81, with three
data orders each (\cref{fig:rank}; AdamW and Muon in \cref{tab:app-am}; SGD with momentum as a reference
in \cref{tab:app-sgd}).

Compared with AdamW's, Muon's weight matrices use their directions more evenly (\cref{tab:app-am}): their stable
rank $\|W\|_F^2/\|W\|_2^2$ is 86 against 33 ($2.61\pm0.04$ times), their effective rank 325 against 272, and their
spectral norm (2.9 against 5.4) and largest-to-median singular-value ratio $\sigma_1/\sigma_{\rm median}$ (2.7 against
8.0) are lower. Consecutive Muon steps are less alike (cosine 0.74 against 0.88); their much higher stable rank (220
against 17) holds by construction, since Muon orthogonalizes every update. Muon also sits about twice as sharp: the
top Hessian eigenvalue $\lambda_{\max}$ is 14 against 6.2, and the trace $\operatorname{tr}H$ 2770 against 1400. But
the two tuned learning rates differ, and across a learning-rate sweep $\lambda_{\max}$ falls about as one over the rate
(log-log slopes $-0.86$ for AdamW and $-0.84$ for Muon, at the peak of the schedule), so part of this gap reflects the
rates rather than the optimizer. AdamW's MLP is sparser: 12\% of its units are active, against 22\%. No prediction
metric separates the two (every AdamW/Muon interval contains 1), and both move equally far from the initialization
(ratio $1.01\pm0.02$): they differ in the shape of the weights, not in how far they travel. SGD differs from both in
most metrics (\cref{tab:app-sgd}).

\paragraph{Step 3: swaps (\cref{fig:swap}).} We swap at nine fork steps from 50 to 4000, in both directions
(\cref{app:forks}). Every swapped run ends closer than two data orders are to both the new optimizer's own run
(0.86--0.95 of the floor) and the parent (0.92--0.98 at fork step 50, 0.20--0.21 at 4000): in its predictions it is
as similar to either run as two data orders are. Its weights are not. It is linearly connected to its parent (barrier below
0.05) only from fork step 2500 when leaving AdamW and never when leaving Muon, against
1000 for a switch to data order 1, and never as close to the new optimizer's own run as that optimizer's data orders
are to each other (third panel). In the final model (right), the one-step updates take the new optimizer's form at
every fork step (stable rank of $\Delta W$ 0.99--1.04 of the way from the parent's value to the new optimizer's),
while the weights and activations keep part of the parent's, the more the later the swap: at fork step 4000, the
stable rank of $W$ moves 0.25 (AdamW to Muon) and 0.46 (Muon to AdamW) of the way, and the fraction of active MLP
units 0.07 and 0.13.
